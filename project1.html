CS 180 Project 1

Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Photo Collection · Kavish Kondap

Overview

Between 1905 and 1915, Sergei Mikhailovich Prokudin-Gorskii travelled around the Russian Empire taking photos of various people and scenery. Since color film was not yet invented, he instead took three separate exposures through different colored filters (red, green, and blue), since projecting all three overlaid could reproduce the original color image.

For this project, my goal was to computationally realign these three frames for any given image taken by Prokudin-Gorskii. As input, we receive a grayscale image with the B, G, and R channels stacked vertically, in that order. Out of curiosity, I tried the naive approach of simply splitting the pane into three and stacking them without any alignment. As seen below, this leads to obvious smearing of the subject of the photo and a blurry image, since the frames are not correctly aligned.

Every image on this page is clickable and opens at full resolution.

One convention before the results. I report a displacement as (x, y) with the origin at the top-left corner, so positive x moves a channel right and positive y moves it down. Blue is always the reference and stays fixed at (0, 0). The numbers listed are what I applied to green and red to bring them onto blue.

Single-Scale Alignment

The most direct approach to align images is to do a grid search over small offsets from the naive alignment above. In other words, if we choose to keep, say, the blue frame fixed, we slide the red and green frames separately on top of the blue frame and check when they are best aligned. Specifically, we can check every integer displacement in the window [-15, 15] × [-15, 15]. If we are dealing with a small image, where the possible displacement falls within this range, this technique will allow us to align the images through a simple grid search of these 961 possible shifts.

Ignoring the borders

I realized while working on this project that all the images in the dataset had a fringe of black/white on the border. Since these fringes are inconsistent across frames, they would count towards the similarity score, and throw off the alignment, especially since they fluctuate between white/black, leading to high contrast between frames. To overcome this, I crop 10% of the width and height from all 4 sides before applying my alignment algorithm in the middle 80% × 80% of the image. For the results, I can keep the original image, and use the offsets found from shifting the cropped images.

The scoring metric

In order to determine if a given shift is “good”, we need a scoring metric. For this project I use two different metrics, L2 distance and NCC. First, for L2, which is the Euclidean distance between two channels:

L2(A, B) = √∑(i,j) ∈ Ω [ A(i,j) − B(i,j) ]2

Next, for normalized cross-correlation, I subtract the mean from each channel, then normalize both and take the dot product.

NCC(A, B) = ∑(i,j) ∈ Ω A′(i,j) · B′(i,j)

Because NCC throws away the mean brightness and the overall contrast, it should in principle handle exposures made through different filters better than L2 does.

Notation. A and B are the two channels being compared, and Ω is the set of pixels where they still overlap once one has been shifted, so the score is only computed over that overlap. A′ and B′ are the mean-subtracted channels rescaled to unit length, so A′ = (A − mean A) / ‖A − mean A‖. A lower L2 means a better match, while NCC goes up as the match improves, so I negate it and minimize either way.

Results

All three low-resolution images align correctly under both metrics, and the two metrics agree on every single offset.

Multi-Scale Pyramid Alignment

For the full-scale TIFF images (around 3700 × 3200 pixels), doing a brute force would require searching pixel-by-pixel over a prohibitively large area due to the large computational cost. Instead, I use an image pyramid to search at different resolutions.

Pyramid Methodology

In order to reduce the computational cost of searching for the optimal alignment, we can take advantage of the fact that searching at a lower resolution gives us a rough idea of where the optimal alignment is. Essentially, if we downscale our image to the size of the images in the single-scale experiments above, we can do our dense [-15, 15] × [-15, 15] search and find a reasonable region for where the optimal alignment is. Then, if we scale back up the resolution, we can search in the area around where the low-res image alignment procedure found its optimal alignment. If we do this in steps, from lowest resolution, and slowly scaling back up to the highest resolution, we can substantially reduce the search cost of alignment.

I implemented this recursively in Python, using scikit-image for scaling down my images with anti-aliasing. Specifically, I would scale the image down by 1/2 on each dimension (aka, the scaled down image is 1/4 the size of the original) repeatedly until both sides of the image were under 512 pixels. Then, I would run my single-scale grid search, and scale back up the image. At the higher resolutions, I initialize my search to be centered around where the lower resolution found the best alignment. With this, the computation drops from around one search with 130,000 candidates to four searches with 961 candidates each, and all of my alignments finish within a minute with L2 and NCC.

Bells & Whistles: Aligning on Gradients

For Emir of Bukhara, the raw brightness of the panes differs substantially due to the vibrant blue of the emir's robe. Specifically, since it is blue, the blue pane has high brightness in that region, whereas the green and red are much less saturated. As a result, the L2/NCC is not indicative of alignment, since the panes themselves are simply different even at perfect alignment.

One way to circumvent this is by aligning on an edge map of each pane rather than the raw pixels. The idea behind this is that, although the saturation of each pane is very different, the edges should be fairly similar, since they are saturation invariant. To do this, I use np.gradient, which returns the partial derivatives, both row-wise and column-wise, and then combine them to get a single gradient magnitude.

‖∇A‖(x,y) = √(∂A/∂x)2 + (∂A/∂y)2

The top row is the three raw exposures. Look at how differently the robe reads in each one: bright in blue, mid gray in green, nearly black in red. The bottom row is the gradient magnitude of each. Notice how the gradients are much more similar and seem to be invariant to the change in saturation that the raw pixels experience.

Before and after

Switching the features from pixels to gradients moves red from (43, 69) to (40, 107), and the ghosting disappears. L2 and NCC also converge to the same solution using the gradient map, rather than the raw pixels.
